\markboth{A Closing Note}{A Closing Note}

The first two chapters of this booklet have taken a familiar word, \emph{data}, and made its assumptions visible. A recorded value is not automatically information, and information is not automatically knowledge. Moving from one to the next requires context, interpretation, and a system in which a recorded difference can matter.

The second chapter has also shown that representation is a relationship, not a property that an object carries by itself. Something can stand for something else only within a context in which that relation can be recognized and used. That leaves a further question at the edge of the discussion: how does a representation become available to a physical system? What allows it to be recorded, distinguished from noise, transmitted, and recovered?

That question is the subject announced by Chapter 3, \emph{From Abstract Representation to Physical Signal}. The English text of that chapter is still a placeholder in this edition. This PDF therefore does not yet contain the full treatment of physical signals, information-theoretic limits, sampling, electrical states, or the physical cost of computation. This is an incomplete English edition, not a complete counterpart to the Persian text.

The distinction matters. A partial edition should not pretend to have answered a question it has only introduced. The two completed chapters establish why data and representation need careful definitions; the missing chapter is where this edition must still explain how those abstractions are realized in matter. Until that translation is complete, the most honest ending is to leave the central question open rather than replace it with a shortened summary.

When does a physical state become data for a system: when it changes, when the change is recorded, when it can be distinguished, or only when a system interprets it as standing for something else?
