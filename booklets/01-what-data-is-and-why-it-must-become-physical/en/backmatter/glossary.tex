This glossary keeps the terminology consistent throughout the book. The definitions are deliberately brief so that readers can consult them while reading; the full discussion of each concept remains in its corresponding chapter.

\section*{Data, Information, and Representation}

\begin{description}
  \item[Data] A difference or content recorded, received, or examined in a particular context. Data do not, by themselves, necessarily amount to understanding or knowledge.
  \item[Information] Content interpreted within a framework that can reduce uncertainty or lack of knowledge. In Shannon's theory, information has a specific technical and probabilistic meaning.
  \item[Knowledge] An organized body of understood and related information that can be used in explanation, reasoning, or action.
  \item[Wisdom] The ability to judge how knowledge should be applied in light of context, consequences, and values.
  \item[Representation] A relation or mechanism through which something stands for or indicates something else within a system.
  \item[Sign] Something that, within an interpretive relation, points to or represents something else.
  \item[Symbol] A sign whose relation to its object relies primarily on convention, rule, or interpretive habit.
  \item[Physical carrier] A material state or process in which a recognizable pattern or difference is realized and can play an informational role within a system.
\end{description}

\section*{Communication and Information Theory}

\begin{description}
  \item[Message] The content intended to travel from a source to a destination; it should not be identified with the physical signal that carries it.
  \item[Source] A system that produces a message or selects one from a set of possible messages.
  \item[Encoding] The conversion of a message into a sequence of symbols or another form suitable for transmission or storage.
  \item[Decoding] The recovery of a message or its symbols from what has been received.
  \item[Channel] The path through which a signal travels from sender to receiver. A channel may be material, optical, acoustic, biological, or a storage medium.
  \item[Noise] Changes or disturbances that make a signal harder to detect or a message harder to reconstruct.
  \item[Probability] A number between zero and one that describes, within a model, the likelihood of an outcome.
  \item[Uncertainty] Lack of knowledge about which outcome will occur among several possibilities; information theory can quantify it through a probability distribution.
  \item[Self-information] The surprise associated with a particular outcome, expressed in Shannon's theory as \(-\log_2 p\).
  \item[Shannon entropy] The average self-information of the outcomes of a random variable; a measure of uncertainty in a probability distribution, not a measure of a message's meaning.
  \item[Channel capacity] The maximum rate at which information can be transmitted reliably under a given channel model.
  \item[Bit] A unit of information corresponding to the choice between two equally likely alternatives; larger information quantities are expressed using the binary logarithm.
  \item[Kolmogorov complexity] The length of the shortest program that produces a string on a reference machine; it is not the same as Shannon entropy.
\end{description}

\section*{Signs, Nature, and Physics}

\begin{description}
  \item[Icon] A sign related to its object through resemblance or a shared quality.
  \item[Index] A sign connected to its object by an actual, causal, or spatial relation; smoke, for example, may indicate fire.
  \item[Natural sign] A naturally occurring trace or state that can provide evidence about another event or condition without being produced with communicative intent.
  \item[Aperiodic solid] Schrödinger's term for a hypothetical structure of hereditary matter whose arrangement is complex and non-repeating, unlike that of a simple crystal.
  \item[Physicalism] A philosophical view according to which reality is ultimately physical, or every fact depends on physical facts.
  \item[Logical state] An abstract value, such as zero or one, interpreted within a logical system.
  \item[Physical state] The actual condition of a material system, describable through quantities such as voltage, charge, fields, or molecular arrangement.
  \item[Maxwell's demon] A thought experiment that raises questions about the relation between information, measurement, selection, and the second law of thermodynamics.
  \item[Landauer's principle] A principle establishing a lower bound on the heat cost of logically irreversible information erasure.
  \item[DNA] A molecule whose sequence of chemical units plays a role in storing and transmitting hereditary information.
\end{description}

\section*{Signals and Electricity}

\begin{description}
  \item[Signal] A detectable physical change or event that can carry a message or information within a specified system.
  \item[Analog signal] A signal whose relevant quantity can vary continuously over a range.
  \item[Digital signal] A signal whose physical variations are interpreted as members of a discrete set of states or symbols.
  \item[Sampling] Measuring a signal at discrete moments in time.
  \item[Aliasing] A phenomenon in which insufficient sampling makes different signal components or signals indistinguishable in the sampled data.
  \item[Quantization] Mapping continuous or highly precise values onto a finite set of discrete levels.
  \item[Electric charge] A physical property of matter involved in electromagnetic interaction and measured in coulombs.
  \item[Electric potential] Electric potential energy per unit charge at a point, relative to a chosen reference.
  \item[Potential difference] The difference in electric potential between two points; it is measured in volts.
  \item[Voltage] The common name for electric potential difference between two points.
  \item[Logic threshold] A boundary used by a circuit or digital system to distinguish ranges corresponding to logical states.
\end{description}
